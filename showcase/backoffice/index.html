Approve the wallet. Never hold the file.

A fund issuer's compliance desk approves and withdraws investor wallets under a four-eyes rule. Nobody at the desk holds an identity document, and nobody holds a key that can sign freely. The investor's EUDI evidence is already proven on chain, the registry refuses any approval without it, and the operator wallet can do exactly two things: approve and revoke.

Who buys, who uses

The issuer that already owns the gate.

A fund issuer, transfer agent or launchpad gates its token behind an allowlist today. That gate is a liability it already runs: an allowlist key, an operations team, and a compliance file that both must touch. It pays for control over the gate, not for a money flow. No money moves through the operator wallet, by design.

This desk sees an address and its evidence. The issuer's compliance file stays with the issuer.

Compliance desk

Decides that an address with evidence may hold the instrument. Holds one P-256 authorization key. Cannot revoke alone, cannot send.

Operations desk

Co-signs approvals. Can withdraw an address alone in an emergency, until a rotation date. Holds one P-256 authorization key.

Neither desk holds the operator's private key. Privy's enclave holds it, and the wallet is owned by the two keys together.

Otherwise not possible

Today, approving an address is an act of document custody.

  1. An address carries nothing, so the person who approves it must be the person who has seen the file, and the key that writes the allowlist sits with that person or with a multisig of such people.

    Source: the product-pitch-legal memo cited in the Attestat product page, and a count of zero live gated products that accept an external attestation without a contract (live-gated-products memo).

  2. With the evidence proven on chain and bound to the address, the approval act needs only the address, the evidence bits and the issuer's own record reference. The registry refuses to approve anything else: attestWithProof is permissionless and never approves, approve is operator-only and reverts NoDecision when no evidence exists.

    Source: contracts/src/AttestationRegistry.sol, lines 114 to 121, and contracts/test/Nachweis.t.sol in the nachweis-app repository.

  3. A plain multisig would still let a majority of its keys mint, move funds or change verifiers. Privy's policy is enforced inside the enclave before signing, and denies by default, so the desk wallet cannot be talked into anything but approve and revoke, even by both of its own keys.

    Source: Privy documentation, controls/policies/overview ("Defaults to DENY if no rules resolve"), fetched 2026-09-09.

How it works

Evidence, then two keys.

Seven beats. Three of them are refusals, because a desk is defined by what it cannot do.

  1. 1

    Presentation

    The investor's official test wallet answers the issuer's registered request with three fields: given name, family name, over 18. Sample identity. The names go to the issuer's verifier, not to the chain.

  2. 2

    Zero-knowledge proof

    The investor's browser tab proves the statement on their own computer and the bridge sends attestWithProof. The chain records evidence: policy, bits, tier, expiry. Nobody is approved yet.

  3. 3

    An intent, raised from the chain

    The back-office service watches the registry's Attested event and proposes an approve intent on the Privy operator wallet. The intent sits at zero of two.

  4. 4

    Two authorizations

    Compliance authorizes with its key: one of two. Operations authorizes with its key: two of two. Privy executes the transaction from the enclave and the registry emits Approved. The fund token and the permissioned pool open on the same record.

  5. 5

    Refused by policy

    Someone tries to send 0.01 ETH from the operator wallet. The policy allows approve and revoke on the registry and nothing else, so Privy refuses before anything is signed.

  6. 6

    Refused by evidence

    Both desks authorize an approval for a stranger address that never brought evidence. The transaction reverts with NoDecision and the intent ends as failed. Two signatures are not enough without evidence.

  7. 7

    Emergency revoke

    Operations alone signs revoke under its time-bound emergency policy. The registry emits Revoked, the fund token refuses the next subscription, the pool checker returns no permission. Manual withdrawal, by hand.

What the chain sees

One record per address. No name in it.

The registry stores one Decision per address and policy. The operator wallet can flip the approval flag and the revoked flag. It cannot write a name, because there is no field for one.

No identity documents on chain, and nothing we could use to find her.

Honest about the demo

  • Official test walletThe German EUDI sandbox with a sample identity. No state-issued ID in this build.
  • Where the proof is madeIn the investor's browser tab, on their own computer. A laptop companion route exists. The official wallet does not produce this proof today.
  • Simulated checksSanctions and similar checks are stubs in this build. Local mode simulates the two-signature count with a plain key on anvil and says so.
  • Manual withdrawalRevocation is triggered by hand. Renewal before expiry is manual in this build.

The Privy part, in Privy's terms

Operator wallet by Privy: a server wallet whose key is reconstituted only inside Privy's secure enclave (Privy's wording), owned by a two-key quorum of the issuer's compliance and operations desks, and bound by a policy that allows approve and revoke on the AttestationRegistry and nothing else. Privy sees this wallet's transactions; it never sees identity evidence.

Two keys on one demo server; in production each desk holds its own key. On the free developer plan, a two-key quorum, a quorum-owned policy and a server wallet were created through the API on 9 September 2026 without a sales contact; the intents API was not used, since the Node SDK has no authorize method and the dashboard has no intents page.

No identity documents on chain, and nothing we could use to find her.
